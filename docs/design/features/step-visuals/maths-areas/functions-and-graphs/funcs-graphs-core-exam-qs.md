## **Functions, Graphs and Core Algebra in Leaving Certificate Higher Level Mathematics**

Examination period: 2015–2026 · Papers 1 and 2 · Research for Puckle's interactive mathematics cards

## **1\. Executive findings**

The central finding is that functions and graphs are not simply an examination topic: they are a way of thinking that students must use throughout Higher Level mathematics.

Even within the narrower scope of core algebra and functions, examiners repeatedly expect students to move between different representations of the same mathematical relationship:

* Formula → graph: What does an algebraic expression tell us about the shape and position of a curve?  
* Graph → formula: What can a graph reveal about roots, coefficients, transformations or an unknown function?  
* Equation → intersections: What does solving an equation mean geometrically?  
* Properties → conclusions: What follows from knowing that a function has a minimum, is one-to-one, or has exactly one root?  
* Function → model: What do the inputs, outputs, restrictions and parameters mean in a real situation?

These are distinct demands. A student may be perfectly competent at factorising a quadratic yet not recognise that its factors specify where its graph crosses the horizontal axis. Equally, a student may know how to substitute values into a formula without understanding how composition or inverse functions work.

For Puckle, that distinction is fundamental: the strongest interactive visuals will establish connections between mathematical representations, not merely demonstrate procedures.

## **2\. Research scope and interpretation**

The report examines the Higher Level papers from 2015 through 2026, using the relevant examination questions alongside the official syllabus and available marking schemes.

The syllabus explicitly requires students to understand composite and inverse functions, the relationships between equations and graphs, completed-square forms of quadratics, polynomial graphs, exponential and logarithmic functions, and graphical solutions of equations. It also emphasises connections between algebraic and graphical representations.

oide.ie+1

I am applying a deliberately stricter inclusion test than a commercial exam-question topic index. A question belongs in this first phase when its important learning demand is about core functions, graphs or algebra. It does not belong merely because a derivative or integral happens to have a function in its notation.

For example, we can analyse the relationship between a quadratic's roots and its graph without teaching the rules of differentiation. Similarly, we can examine the meaning of an exponential model without turning the report into a chapter on financial mathematics.

Phase 2 remains separate: calculus, complex numbers, trigonometry, coordinate geometry and other topics will be examined specifically for their dependence on the foundations identified here, after you review this report.

## **3\. How examination questions are framed**

It is useful to classify questions in two different ways: the mathematics being tested and the kind of thinking the wording demands.

A question about a quadratic function can require straightforward substitution, algebraic solving, graphical interpretation, proof, or reasoning about an unknown parameter. Teaching the formula without teaching students to recognise these different demands would leave an important gap.

| Examination task | What students must recognise | Representative references |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Evaluate a function | An input determines an output; substitution must respect the domain | 2026 P1 Q8(a); 2025 P1 Q2(b) |
| Construct a graph | A table of values represents points belonging to the same function | 2015 P1 Q8(a); 2016 P1 Q3(a) |
| Interpret a graph | Coordinates, intersections, extrema and shape carry mathematical information | 2018 P1 Q6(a); 2024 P1 Q4(c) |
| Solve an equation | Roots of $f(x)=0$ are x-intercepts; solutions of $f(x)=g(x)$ are intersections | 2016 P1 Q3(b); 2023 P1 Q6(a) |
| Explain why | A conclusion must follow from a property of the function, not merely a calculation | 2017 P1 Q1(c); 2024 P1 Q4(c) |
| Find unknown parameters | Coefficients control a graph's position, shape, zeros or behaviour | 2023 P1 Q2(a); 2025 P1 Q6(b) |
| Transform a graph | Changing a function's inputs differs from changing its outputs | 2023 P1 Q2(c); 2026 P1 Q10(a) |
| Compose or invert | Functions are mappings that can be chained or reversed under suitable conditions | 2023 P1 Q5(c); 2025 P1 Q2(b) |
| Interpret a model | Variables and parameters have real-world meanings and restrictions | 2017 P1 Q7; 2021 P1 Q9 |
| Test a statement | A property may be true locally but false globally, or depend on the domain | 2024 P1 Q4(c); 2026 P1 Q10(f) |

These are all genuine examination framings found in the papers.

### **The underlying knowledge is often more important than the calculation**

Consider three different instructions:

$f(x)=0,f(x)=g(x),f(x)>0.$

A student needs to interpret these as three different graphical questions.

$f(x)=0$

Where does the curve meet the x-axis?

$f(x)=g(x)$

Where do the two graphs meet?

$f(x)>0$

Where is the graph above the x-axis?  
Conceptual illustrations only; these are not reproductions of an examination diagram.

A student who understands these connections can recognise the mathematics even when the examiner changes the wording, introduces two different functions, or embeds the equation in a real-world story.

## **4\. Detailed examination case studies**

The following examples are selected because they expose important relationships that Puckle could teach visually. They are condensed reproductions of the mathematical tasks, with working and interpretation; they are not intended to reproduce every word of the original questions.

### **4.1 Quadratics: one function, three representations**

2017 · Paper 1 · Question 1(a–c)

The question gives:

$f(x)=2{x}^{2}-7x-10$

Students complete the square, identify its minimum point, explain why it has two real roots, and calculate those roots.

Working

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} f(x)&=2\\left(x^2-\\frac72x\\right)-10\\\\ &=2\\left(x-\\frac74\\right)^2-\\frac{129}{8}. \\end{aligned} \\\]

Therefore its vertex is:

\\\[ \\boxed{\\left(\\frac74,-\\frac{129}{8}\\right)} \\\]

Since the coefficient of the squared term is positive, the parabola opens upwards. Its minimum value is negative, so it must cross the x-axis twice.

Solving $f(x)=0$:

\\\[ \\boxed{x=\\frac{7\\pm\\sqrt{129}}4} \\\]

The conceptual significance is that completing the square tells us about the graph before we solve the equation. We know the number of roots because of the shape and position of the parabola.

This is substantially different from completing the square as an unexplained algebraic procedure.

### **4.2 A double root means one distinct x-intercept**

2018 · Paper 1 · Question 2(b)

Students investigate the polynomial:

$f(x)={x}^{3}-17{x}^{2}+80x-64.$

They establish that $x=1$ is a root and find the remaining roots.

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} f(x)&=(x-1)(x^2-16x+64)\\\\ &=\\boxed{(x-1)(x-8)^2}. \\end{aligned} \\\]

The distinct real roots are $1$ and $8$, with $8$ a repeated root.

The graph crosses the x-axis at $x=1$, but touches and turns at $x=8$.

This question demonstrates an important distinction between the degree of a polynomial, the number of roots counted with multiplicity, and the number of different x-intercepts.

Visual opportunity: Compare a single factor $(x-r)$ with a squared factor $(x-r{)}^{2}$, showing how the graph's behaviour at the root changes.

### **4.3 The discriminant is a statement about a graph**

2025 · Paper 1 · Question 6(b)

The examination gives:

$h(x)=6m{x}^{2}-4rx+54m,m,r>0.$

Students determine a condition on $r$ and $m$ for the quadratic equation $h(x)=0$ to have exactly one solution.

For one distinct real root, the discriminant must be zero:

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} (-4r)^2-4(6m)(54m)&=0\\\\ 16r^2-1296m^2&=0\\\\ r^2&=81m^2. \\end{aligned} \\\]

Since both parameters are positive:

\\\[ \\boxed{r=9m}. \\\]

Substituting this back gives:

$h(x)=6m(x-3{)}^{2}.$

So the parabola touches the x-axis at $x=3$, but does not cross it.

The examination tests the link:

\\\[ \\text{discriminant }=0 \\quad\\Longleftrightarrow\\quad \\text{one distinct real root} \\quad\\Longleftrightarrow\\quad \\text{tangent to the x-axis}. \\\]

The published marking scheme confirms algebraic approaches to this condition.

For this first phase, the important visual experience is the change in the number of real graph intersections; complex roots can be developed in Phase 2\.

### **4.4 Solving two functions means finding where they intersect**

2018 · Paper 1 · Question 6(a)

The paper shows two curves:

$h(x)=x,k(x)={x}^{3}$

and asks for their intersection coordinates.

Equate their outputs:

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} x&=x^3\\\\ x(x^2-1)&=0\\\\ x(x-1)(x+1)&=0. \\end{aligned} \\\]

Therefore:

\\\[ \\boxed{(-1,-1),\\ (0,0),\\ (1,1)}. \\\]

This has two conceptual steps: finding the inputs where the outputs agree, then finding the corresponding output for each point. It is not sufficient to give only the three x-values when coordinates are requested.

The question presents the graphs and requires students to connect that visual information with algebraic solving.

A similar but structurally different example appears in 2023 Paper 1 Question 6(a)(i):

$f(x)=x+4,g(x)={x}^{2}-2.$  
\\\[ x+4=x^2-2 \\Longrightarrow (x-3)(x+2)=0. \\\]

The intersections are:

\\\[ \\boxed{(-2,2),\\ (3,7)}. \\\]

Here the student must recognise that the equation is about the intersection of a line and a parabola.

### **4.5 Algebraic manipulation can introduce false solutions**

2015 · Paper 1 · Question 5(a)

The question asks students to solve:

$x=\sqrt{x+6}.$

Squaring both sides gives:

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} x^2&=x+6\\\\ (x-3)(x+2)&=0. \\end{aligned} \\\]

This produces two candidates, $x=3$ and $x=-2$.

But:

$-2\ne \sqrt{4}.$

So the only actual solution is:

\\\[ \\boxed{x=3}. \\\]

The mathematical issue is not factorisation. It is that squaring both sides can lose information about the signs of the original expressions. Squaring is not reversible in the way required to preserve the exact solution set.

Visual opportunity: Graph $y=x$ and $y=\sqrt{x+6}$. Only their genuine intersection should count. Separately show why the squared equation also permits $x=-2$.

### **4.6 Logarithmic domain restrictions can also eliminate a root**

2019 · Paper 1 · Question 3(a–b)

Students first factorise:

$3xy-9x+4y-12=(3x+4)(y-3).$

Then the question uses $y=\ln x$, producing:

$g(x)=(3x+4)(\ln x-3).$

To solve $g(x)=0$, students might obtain:

$x=-\frac{4}{3}orx={e}^{3}.$

But $\ln x$ is only defined for $x>0$, so $-4/3$ is not admissible.

Thus:

\\\[ \\boxed{x=e^3}. \\\]

Underlying concept: A solution must belong to the domain of the original function. Algebraic factors cannot override that restriction.

Visual opportunity: Show the logarithmic curve existing only to the right of the y-axis, with a candidate root in the forbidden region visibly rejected.

### **4.7 An inequality describes an interval or region, not just a number**

2018 · Paper 1 · Question 1(b)

Students solve:

$\frac{2x-3}{x+2}\leq 3.$

Rearranging:

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} \\frac{-x-9}{x+2}&\\leq0\\\\ \\frac{x+9}{x+2}&\\geq0. \\end{aligned} \\\]

The important boundary points are $x=-9$, where the numerator vanishes, and $x=-2$, where the expression is undefined.

A sign analysis gives:

\\\[ \\boxed{x\\leq-9\\quad\\text{or}\\quad x\>-2}. \\\]

The common algebraic trap is to multiply by $x+2$ without knowing whether it is positive or negative; multiplying an inequality by a negative number reverses its direction.

Visual opportunity: Link a sign chart on the number line to the graph of the rational function, including its vertical asymptote and the exclusion of $x=-2$.

### **4.8 Graph transformations: the direction is the conceptual challenge**

2023 · Paper 1 · Question 2(c)

Students are given a graph of $g(x)$, with domain:

$-2\leq x\leq 2.$

They must draw two new graphs.

First transformation:

$y=g(x)-2$

This moves every point down two units. The input values are unchanged, so the new domain remains:

\\\[ \\boxed{\[-2,2\]}. \\\]

Second transformation:

$y=g(x+3)$

This moves every point three units left. But the examination goes further: students must draw it over its largest possible domain.

Since the input $x+3$ must belong to the original domain:

$-2\leq x+3\leq 2,$

the transformed domain is:

\\\[ \\boxed{-5\\leq x\\leq-1}. \\\]

This is a particularly strong examination example because it tests both the direction of a transformation and its effect on the allowable inputs.

2026 · Paper 1 · Question 10(a)

The exam returns to this distinction with drawings of $g(x)$, $g(x)+1$, $h(x)$ and $h(x-1)$. Students then draw $g(x)+3$ and $h(x+2)$.

The distinction is:

\\\[ \\begin{array}{ll} g(x)+3 & \\text{up 3}\\\\ h(x+2) & \\text{left 2}\\\\ h(x-1) & \\text{right 1}. \\end{array} \\\]

The question expects students to recognise transformations directly from the notation and supplied graphs, without necessarily having a formula for the functions.

### **4.9 Function composition and inverse functions from a graph**

2025 · Paper 1 · Question 2(b)(ii–iii)

This is one of the clearest recent examples of functions being examined as mathematical objects.

The supplied graph consists of two line segments joining:

\\\[ (0,0)\\longrightarrow(3,1)\\longrightarrow(4,4). \\\]

The student must find $g(g(3))$.

Reading the graph:

$g(3)=1.$

So:

\\\[ g(g(3))=g(1)=\\boxed{\\frac13}. \\\]

The next part asks students to draw the inverse function.

Its graph contains the corresponding points:

\\\[ (0,0)\\longrightarrow(1,3)\\longrightarrow(4,4), \\\]

because an inverse exchanges the input and output coordinates:

\\\[ (x,y)\\longleftrightarrow(y,x). \\\]

Graphically, this is a reflection in the line $y=x$. The function is strictly increasing on the specified domain, so its inverse is itself a function.

The central conceptual leap is that function composition is repeated input–output processing, while inversion reverses that processing. Neither operation is merely a manipulation of symbols.

Visual opportunity: Use an input-output trace: highlight $x=3$, follow it to output 1, feed 1 back as the next input, and locate $1/3$. Then swap input and output axes to reveal the inverse.

### **4.10 One-to-one and onto are different properties**

2023 · Paper 1 · Question 5(c)

Students examine a diagram containing functions:

$f:A\rightarrow B,g:B\rightarrow C,$

where sets $A$ and $C$ have four elements each, while $B$ has three.

They evaluate $g(f(3))$ by following two arrows, and explain why $g$ is injective but not surjective.

The diagram shows that the three inputs to $g$ have three different outputs. Therefore $g$ is injective. But $C$ contains four elements, with one not reached at all. Therefore $g$ is not surjective.

The important knowledge is:

* Injective: different inputs do not share the same output.  
* Surjective: every element of the stated codomain is reached.

The second property depends on the codomain, not only on the graph or list of outputs.

Visual opportunity: Allow inputs to connect to outputs, making collisions and unreached codomain elements immediately apparent. Connect this to the horizontal-line test for injectivity of real-valued functions.

### **4.11 Algebraic composition is also examined**

2024 · Paper 1 · Question 6(b)

Two functions are given for positive $x$:

$f(x)={e}^{9x},g(x)=\ln \sqrt{x}.$

The question includes evaluating the functions and simplifying the composition $g(f(x))$.

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} g(f(x)) &=\\ln\\sqrt{e^{9x}}\\\\ &=\\ln\\left(e^{9x/2}\\right)\\\\ &=\\boxed{\\frac92x}. \\end{aligned} \\\]

This depends on understanding that $f(x)$ is first calculated and then used as the input to $g$. It also relies on the inverse relationship between exponential and logarithmic functions.

The notation $g(f(x))$ is sometimes experienced as a complicated algebraic expression. Conceptually, it means apply one function, then another.

### **4.12 Exponential functions: a graphical solution and an exact solution**

2016 · Paper 1 · Question 3(a–b)

The paper gives:

$f(x)=\frac{2}{{e}^{x}},g(x)={e}^{x}-1.$

Students complete a table, draw both graphs for $0\leq x\leq \ln 4$, estimate the intersection, and then solve $f(x)=g(x)$ algebraically.

$\frac{2}{{e}^{x}}={e}^{x}-1.$

Let $u={e}^{x}$, where $u>0$:

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} \\frac2u&=u-1\\\\ 2&=u^2-u\\\\ (u-2)(u+1)&=0. \\end{aligned} \\\]

The algebra gives $u=2$ or $u=-1$. The negative value is impossible for ${e}^{x}$, so:

\\\[ \\boxed{x=\\ln2\\approx0.693}. \\\]

This is an exceptionally useful example for visual design because the examiner explicitly requires both graphical estimation and algebraic solving of the same intersection.

The lesson is not only how to solve an exponential equation. It is that a graphical intersection represents a mathematical equality, and a substitution such as $u={e}^{x}$ must preserve the restrictions of the original function.

Visual opportunity: Show the two curves approaching their intersection, then translate the same equality into an equation in $u$. Retain the fact that $u$ is a positive exponential output; the algebra must not make that constraint disappear.

### **4.13 Exponential substitution inside a quadratic equation**

2021 · Paper 1 · Question 3(b)

The exam first asks students to solve:

$3{x}^{2}+8x-35=0.$

Then it uses the same structure inside an exponential equation:

${3}^{2m+1}=35-8({3}^{m}).$

Put $u={3}^{m}$:

$3{u}^{2}+8u-35=0$

and factor:

$(3u-7)(u+5)=0.$

Because ${3}^{m}>0$, $u=-5$ is impossible. Therefore:

\\\[ 3^m=\\frac73 \\quad\\Longrightarrow\\quad \\boxed{m=\\log\_3\\\!\\left(\\frac73\\right)}. \\\]

The examiner is testing whether students can recognise a quadratic structure hidden inside an exponential equation. This is a more sophisticated form of pattern recognition than routine substitution.

### **4.14 Exponential models: intercepts, asymptotes and interpretation**

2021 · Paper 1 · Question 9(a–b)

A cooling model is given:

$T(t)=A{e}^{-0.081t}+20.$

The coffee starts at \\(95^\\circ\\text{C}\\), so:

\\\[ 95=A+20\\quad\\Longrightarrow\\quad A=75. \\\]

Thus:

$T(t)=75{e}^{-0.081t}+20.$

The paper explicitly asks students to explain the constant $20$.

It is the temperature the coffee is approaching as it cools: the model's limiting ambient temperature. Graphically, the line $T=20$ is a horizontal asymptote.

To find when the coffee reaches \\(82^\\circ\\text{C}\\):

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} 75e^{-0.081t}+20&=82\\\\ e^{-0.081t}&=\\frac{62}{75}\\\\ t&=\\frac{\\ln(62/75)}{-0.081}. \\end{aligned} \\\]

So:

\\\[ \\boxed{t\\approx2.35\\text{ minutes}}. \\\]

The conceptual demands are recognising an initial value, a limiting value, exponential decay and the use of logarithms to recover an input from an output.

Visual opportunity: Display $A$, the initial temperature $A+20$, and the asymptote 20 as related quantities. Show why changing $A$ changes the starting temperature without changing the long-term limit.

### **4.15 Parameters and graph symmetry in real-world modelling**

2025 · Paper 1 · Question 9(a)(i)

A car's fuel consumption is modelled by:

$F(c)=0.05{c}^{2}-8.5c+800,40\leq c\leq 120,$

where $c$ is speed in km/h.

The paper asks students to show that fuel consumption at 60 km/h equals that at 110 km/h.

$F(60)=470,F(110)=470.$

Why do these two speeds have the same output?

The axis of symmetry is:

\\\[ c=\\frac{8.5}{0.1}=\\boxed{85}. \\\]

The speeds 60 and 110 are each 25 units from 85\. A parabola has equal heights at inputs equally far from its axis of symmetry.

The minimum occurs at $c=85$, where:

$F(85)=438.75.$

The original question primarily asks for substitution, but the symmetry provides an important conceptual explanation for the result.

### **4.16 Real-world input restrictions change the answers**

2021 · Paper 1 · Question 10(a)(i)

The volume of water in a tank is modelled by:

$V(t)=60+41t-3{t}^{2},$

where $t$ measures days from day zero.

To determine when the tank is empty:

\\\[ \\begin{aligned} 60+41t-3t^2&=0\\\\ 3t^2-41t-60&=0\\\\ t&=\\frac{41\\pm49}{6}. \\end{aligned} \\\]

The algebraic roots are $15$ and $-4/3$.

Only the nonnegative time is relevant to the real-world situation, so the answer is:

\\\[ \\boxed{15\\text{ days}}. \\\]

The negative root remains a genuine root of the polynomial. It is excluded because of the interpretation of $t$, not because the algebra was incorrect.

That distinction matters: a mathematical function can have a larger natural domain than the real-world model for which it is being used.

### **4.17 A local minimum is not necessarily a global minimum**

2024 · Paper 1 · Question 4(c)

The examination describes a continuous function with a local minimum at $(0,5)$. Students must decide whether the function must be at least 5 everywhere.

The answer is no.

A local minimum tells us what happens in a neighbourhood of a point, not across the entire domain.

For example, a continuous function could have a small valley at height 5 near $x=0$, then descend to a lower value much farther away.

An explicit counterexample is:

$h(x)=5+{x}^{2}-{x}^{4}.$

It has a local minimum at $(0,5)$, but:

$h(2)=5+4-16=-7.$

This is an unusually pure test of conceptual understanding: no differentiation calculation is required to appreciate why the proposed conclusion is invalid.

archive.maths.nuim.ie

Visual opportunity: Show a narrow window around a local minimum, then widen the viewing window to reveal lower values elsewhere. The meaning of local becomes visible.

### **4.18 Periodicity need not involve trigonometric functions**

2026 · Paper 1 · Question 10(b)

The examination provides a periodic graph built from straight-line segments and asks students for its period and for nonzero integer values of $n$ such that:

$f(x+n)=f(x).$

The pattern repeats every 5 units, so:

\\\[ \\boxed{\\text{period}=5}. \\\]

Examples include:

\\\[ \\boxed{n=-5,\\ 5,\\ 10}. \\\]

This is important because students can learn what periodicity means before encountering sine and cosine functions.

A periodic function is defined by repeated output behaviour under a fixed change of input. The graphical repetition is the primary idea; trigonometric functions are particular examples of it.

Visual opportunity: Slide one copy of a periodic graph horizontally across another until their shapes coincide. Display the identity $f(x+5)=f(x)$ as a description of that coincidence.

### **4.19 An equation that is true for every input is different from an equation to be solved**

2026 · Paper 1 · Question 10(f)

Students investigate functions of the form:

$h(x)=a{x}^{2}+bx+c$

satisfying:

$h(x+1)=h(x)+2$

for all real $x$.

Expand the left side:

$h(x+1)=a{x}^{2}+(2a+b)x+(a+b+c).$

The right side is:

$h(x)+2=a{x}^{2}+bx+c+2.$

For the expressions to be identical for every input, the coefficients must agree:

\\\[ 2a+b=b\\quad\\Longrightarrow\\quad a=0, \\\]

and:

\\\[ a+b+c=c+2\\quad\\Longrightarrow\\quad b=2. \\\]

Therefore, within the specified polynomial family:

\\\[ \\boxed{h(x)=2x+c}. \\\]

The graph must be a straight line with gradient 2\.

This is not just a coefficient-matching task. It expresses a relationship between horizontal and vertical changes: every increase of 1 in the input produces an increase of 2 in the output.

A subtle but important qualification: this does not prove that every conceivable function satisfying the identity is linear. The examination first restricts attention to the stated polynomial family.

Visual opportunity: Move a point one unit to the right and show that it must rise by two. Then let students investigate why a genuinely quadratic curve cannot maintain that same change everywhere.

### **4.20 An especially useful parameter question**

2020 · Paper 1 · Question 1(a)

This question deserves particular attention because it tests several connected ideas using the same quadratic function:

$f(x)={x}^{2}+5x+p,$

where $p$ is an integer from $-3$ to $8$.

Students must find:

1. The value of $p$ for which $x+3$ is a factor.  
2. The value of $p$ for which the roots differ by 3\.  
3. The two values of $p$ for which the graph does not cross the x-axis.

All three can be understood as different conditions on the same parabola.

| Condition | Mathematical reasoning | Answer |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| $x+3$ is a factor | $f(-3)=0$, so $9-15+p=0$ | $p=6$ |
| Roots differ by 3 | Root separation is $\sqrt{25-4p}$, so $25-4p=9$ | $p=4$ |
| Graph does not cross the x-axis | Discriminant $25-4p\leq 0$; apply the specified integer restriction | $p=7,8$ |

The vertex moves vertically as $p$ changes. That one change determines whether the graph crosses the x-axis twice, touches it, or misses it entirely.

This is arguably one of the best examples in the whole research period of why students need a connected understanding of quadratics rather than several independent algebraic recipes.

archive.maths.nuim.ie

Visual opportunity: Keep a single parabola and vary $p$, displaying the root positions, discriminant and factor condition simultaneously. This would make three apparently different examination questions recognisable as variations on one mathematical relationship.

### **Five particularly important conceptual leaps**

Leap 1: An equation is a statement about two outputs being equal.

This explains why roots, graph intersections and substitution methods are closely connected.

Leap 2: An algebraic operation may change the candidate solution set.

Squaring, multiplying by expressions containing a variable, and substituting logarithmic or exponential expressions require attention to domains and equivalence.

Leap 3: A function's formula has geometric structure.

Factors, completed squares, coefficients and discriminants encode properties of its graph.

Leap 4: Inputs and outputs play different roles.

Composition, inversion and graph transformations become easier when the student sees exactly which quantity is changing.

Leap 5: A graph represents a relationship over a domain, not merely an isolated picture.

Endpoints, missing values, asymptotes, continuity, local behaviour and modelling restrictions all depend on that idea.

## **7\. Examination prevalence and conceptual importance**

There is a significant methodological limitation to any attempt to calculate how often a topic appears.

A question may simultaneously test several skills. For example, 2016 P1 Q3 tests exponential functions, graph plotting, intersections, substitution, solving equations and domain restrictions. Counting it as one “exponential question” conceals most of its educational demands.

Likewise, an entire calculus question may rely on knowledge of turning points or graph behaviour without explicitly asking the candidate to explain that knowledge.

Therefore I recommend not assigning invented frequency percentages based on topical labels.

### **An important design principle**

These concepts should not all be turned into equally complicated interactive experiences.

For example, understanding that $g(x)+3$ shifts a graph upward is relatively simple. It may need freedom for students to explore and observe.

Understanding why a squared equation introduces extraneous roots is more subtle. That may benefit from a carefully structured comparison between the original and transformed equations, with a clearly defined reveal moment.

The same principle applies to factorisation: the visual goal is not merely to show a curve and an equation together, but to make the relationship between a factor and a root unmistakable.

### **What the visual-design chat should preserve**

I recommend that each future visual candidate explicitly identify:

| Design requirement | Question to answer |
| ----- | ----- |
| Conceptual claim | What mathematical relationship will become understandable? |
| Prior knowledge | What must the student already know? |
| Representation change | Which algebraic, graphical or verbal forms are being connected? |
| Likely misconception | What wrong interpretation is the activity designed to expose? |
| Interaction purpose | What can the student change, and why is changing it informative? |
| Reveal moment | What insight should follow from observing the change? |
| Exam transfer | Which real examination questions become easier to recognise? |

This should prevent the visuals from becoming either conventional worked-solution animations or open-ended graph playgrounds without a precise learning purpose.

## **10\. Research conclusions and recommendation**

The examination papers show that Higher Level students need more than the ability to manipulate algebraic expressions. They are expected to understand how those expressions encode relationships, restrictions and properties.

The most important recurring connections are:

1. A root is an intersection, and a factor explains why that intersection occurs.  
2. A quadratic's algebraic form determines its geometry.  
3. The domain is part of the meaning of a function, not an optional technical detail.  
4. Changing the input differs from changing the output.  
5. Composition and inversion are actions on mappings, not arbitrary symbol manipulation.  
6. Exponential and logarithmic functions are connected through the reversal of an input–output relationship.  
7. Mathematical conclusions depend on the domain and on whether a statement is local, global or universal.

### **Recommended starting point for the main chat**

I would take the following five conceptual foundations into the first design review:

1\. Functions, equations and intersections

The broadest conceptual foundation, useful everywhere from algebra to calculus.

2\. The three forms of a quadratic

A powerful connection between procedures students already know and the geometry behind them.

3\. Domains, restrictions and valid solutions

Repeatedly examined and often invisible in a purely procedural explanation.

4\. Transformations of functions

Particularly well suited to direct manipulation, with strong examination evidence from recent years.

5\. Composition and inverse functions

An area where a visual understanding of input-output relationships is especially valuable.

These are not necessarily five cards. Each could support several cards, and there are other important topics identified throughout the report. They are simply the strongest candidates for foundational visual learning experiences before the broader content is organised.

### **Final methodological note**

This report contains a broad, source-checked analysis with more than 50 referenced examination tasks and subparts and detailed working for the most revealing examples.

It is not a statistically exhaustive classification of every subpart in every Paper 1 and Paper 2 from 2015–2026. Producing exact annual occurrence counts would require a separate, explicit question-by-question coding exercise, particularly where a task blends algebra with calculus or another strand.
